Fiscal policy

Fiscal policy uses government taxes and spending to influence the economy. Congress passes federal tax and spending laws; the president may sign or veto them, and Congress may override a veto.

Expansionary: lower taxes and/or increase spending to support demand, growth, and jobs during a recession. Lower taxes leave households with more disposable income—the income left after personal taxes. Government purchases, such as school or highway repairs, directly add spending and support jobs. Assistance payments can help recipients continue buying goods.

Contractionary: raise taxes and/or reduce spending to slow demand and ease inflation pressure. Higher taxes leave less after-tax income; fewer public purchases reduce total demand. These are intended effects, not guarantees of exact results.

A tax deduction reduces the income subject to tax. A tax credit directly reduces tax owed. Paying for a public project is spending; changing a deduction is tax policy.

Monetary policy

The Federal Reserve conducts monetary policy to influence money, interest rates, and credit conditions. Expansionary policy supports borrowing, investment, and spending. Contractionary policy restrains borrowing and spending to ease inflation pressure.

Discount rate: the rate banks pay to borrow directly from the Fed. Lowering it makes that borrowing cheaper; raising it makes that borrowing more expensive. It differs from the federal funds rate between banks.

Reserve requirement: the share of deposits banks must hold in reserve. In the historical textbook model, lowering it frees potential lending capacity; raising it leaves less available for lending. Actual U.S. reserve requirement ratios have been zero since March 2020. This activity uses the historical model to practice the tool.

Open-market operations: Fed purchases and sales of government securities. Purchases add bank reserves and support lending in the textbook model. Sales remove reserves and restrain lending.

All expansionary: lower discount rate, lower reserve requirement, buy bonds. All contractionary: raise discount rate, raise reserve requirement, sell bonds. A tool that stays unchanged should be set to 0. Increases in borrowing costs tend to reduce borrowing; decreases tend to encourage it.

Regulation

Government regulation sets enforceable rules for economic activity. Safety tests for medicines, pollution controls, and required food labels are examples.

Benefits can include safer products, cleaner air, improved public health, and better-informed consumers. Businesses can face costs for equipment, testing, paperwork, or other compliance work. A rule may protect the public while raising production costs. Its benefits and costs must both be considered.
