CS 1: Civic action plans

Civic engagement includes researching a community issue, discussing possible responses, and participating in government decisions. Define the problem using evidence, identify the body with authority, compare possible solutions and their costs and benefits, consider intended and unintended consequences, and make an action plan. A plan identifies tasks, responsible people, deadlines, and a way to evaluate results. Political processes involve gaining governmental power or influencing decisions; public policy processes concern the institutional actions and procedures government uses to perform its functions. A public hearing or comment period allows input but does not transfer officials’ legal authority to the participants.

Civic engagement
Taking part in addressing community issues and influencing government decisions.
Political process
Activities for gaining government power or influencing government decisions.
Public policy process
Government procedures and actions used to carry out public functions.
Civic issue
A community problem or concern that may call for collective or government action.
Action plan
A sequence of tasks, responsibilities, and deadlines for addressing a problem.
Cost-benefit analysis
Comparing the disadvantages and advantages of possible courses of action.
Consequence
An outcome that can result from a decision, whether intended or unintended.
Jurisdiction
The area or subject over which a government body has authority.
Public hearing
A government meeting that allows people to present views on a proposed action.
Public comment
Input that people submit to a government body about a policy or proposal.

CS 2: Parties, interest groups, and media

Political parties organize people with similar views and seek to influence policy by winning elections and holding office. Interest groups organize around shared concerns and try to influence policy; they do not have to run their own candidates. Both can recruit volunteers, communicate through media, and encourage civic participation. Choose a party or interest group whose priorities fit the issue and defend that choice. Match the communication channel to the audience you need to reach. A large audience alone does not establish effectiveness. Coalitions bring groups together around a shared objective even when they differ on other questions.

Political party
An organization seeking to influence policy by winning elections and holding office.
Interest group
An organization seeking to influence policy around shared interests or concerns.
Media
Channels of mass communication that reach particular audiences.
Party platform
A statement of the policies and goals a political party supports.
Campaign
An organized effort to persuade voters to support a candidate or ballot proposal.
Lobbying
Communicating with government officials to influence public policy decisions.
Advocacy
Publicly supporting a cause or a proposed course of action.
Grassroots organizing
Building participation among ordinary community members to support a cause.
Target audience
The particular people a message is designed to reach and influence.
Coalition
An alliance of groups working together toward a shared objective.

CS 3: Evaluating sources

A relevant source addresses the question being researched. A credible source deserves trust after evaluation, rather than because it has a polished design or many followers. Check the author’s relevant qualifications and reputation, when and why the material was created, its methods and supporting evidence, the logic of its conclusions, its internal consistency, and its agreement with independent credible sources. Identify perspective, potential bias, funding, and unstated assumptions. Bias does not automatically make every fact false, and agreement among copied reports is not independent corroboration. Historical sources may illuminate earlier conditions without proving present conditions.

Credibility
The degree to which information or its source deserves trust.
Relevance
The degree to which information addresses the specific question being researched.
Evidence
Facts, observations, or data used to support a claim or conclusion.
Bias
A tendency or preference that may influence how information is selected or presented.
Perspective
A viewpoint shaped by a source’s experiences, position, or interests.
Corroboration
Checking a claim against independent sources to see whether they support it.
Internal consistency
Agreement among the statements and data within the same source.
Qualification
Training, experience, or expertise relevant to the topic a source discusses.
Assumption
An idea accepted as true without being demonstrated in the argument.
Context
The circumstances in which a source was created and should be interpreted.

CS 4: Reaching democratic agreement

Persuasion uses reasons and evidence to encourage acceptance of a position. Negotiation is discussion of proposals and terms to reach agreement. Compromise resolves differences through concessions. Consensus building seeks broad support through participation and attention to concerns; it does not require everyone to begin with the same opinion and is not simply winning a majority vote. These processes can work together: participants listen, identify common ground, negotiate terms, persuade with evidence, and compromise on details. Examine trade-offs and involve stakeholders whose experiences help reveal effects. Consensus is not a promise that every disagreement disappears.

Persuasion
Using reasons and evidence to encourage others to accept a position.
Compromise
Settling a disagreement through concessions by the parties involved.
Consensus building
Developing a decision participants can broadly support through shared discussion.
Negotiation
Discussing proposals and terms with others to reach an agreement.
Concession
Giving up part of a demand to help reach an agreement.
Common ground
Interests or goals that people with different positions share.
Active listening
Giving careful attention to another person and checking your understanding.
Deliberation
Carefully considering evidence and alternatives before making a group decision.
Trade-off
Accepting less of one benefit to gain more of another.
Stakeholder
A person or group affected by or interested in a decision.
