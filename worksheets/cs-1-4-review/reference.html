CS 1 • Define the problem

Civic action begins with a specific concern. Describe the condition, the affected people and the location. Separate observations about the problem from a preferred remedy: “buses cannot safely stop here” identifies a concern; “buy a new bus” already chooses a solution. A clear definition helps determine what evidence to collect.

CS 1 • Research before choosing

Research gathers relevant information: official records, observations, surveys and comparisons. Debate examines competing arguments about what should be done. Evidence should answer the local question being studied. For example, planning a new library branch calls for information about neighborhood access and likely use, rather than statistics about an unrelated service.

CS 1 • Build an action plan

An action plan connects a goal with practical steps. Identify who will collect evidence, contact decision makers, communicate with affected residents and complete each task. Assign responsibilities and deadlines. Implementing the plan means carrying out those steps rather than only describing the desired final result.

CS 1 • Weigh consequences, costs and benefits

Compare expected gains with the money, time and other resources a proposal requires. Consider who benefits and who may bear a burden. Also investigate unintended effects. A new public facility might improve access but change traffic nearby. A drawback does not automatically defeat a proposal; it must be considered alongside benefits and alternatives.

CS 1 • Political and public policy processes

Political processes concern gaining governmental power and influencing government decisions; elections and candidate campaigns are clear examples of selecting who governs. Public policy processes concern institutions carrying out their functions, such as considering ordinances, issuing regulations or administering programs. These activities can overlap. Identify the specific action and purpose instead of assuming all advocacy is an election campaign.

CS 1 • Choose the responsible institution

Direct civic requests to an institution with responsibility for the issue. Investigate whether a city, county, school district, state agency or national institution can take the requested action. Public support matters, but it does not create powers an institution lacks. Jurisdiction and the relevant procedure help make an action plan practical.

CS 1 • Evaluate after implementation

After action is taken, compare results with the original goal. Examine evidence about effectiveness, costs and unexpected consequences. Use those findings to consider adjustments. Approval of a proposal is not the same as proof that it solved the problem.

CS 2 • Parties and interest groups

Political parties organize people around public issues and seek to win elections so their members hold office. Interest groups organize shared concerns to influence how policy is made or carried out. Both can communicate, encourage participation and support candidates. Their main goals distinguish them; endorsing a candidate does not by itself make an organization a party. Government agencies, courts and election boards have different official duties.

CS 2 • Match media to audience and purpose

Media are means of communicating with audiences. Choose a method that reaches the relevant people with information they can use. A local announcement may publicize an event; a detailed briefing may help officials assess a proposal. Consider geography, timing, accessibility and cost. A message does not gain lawmaking authority simply because many people hear it.

CS 2 • Defend a viable civic strategy

Connect the organization’s goal, the responsible decision makers, the audience and a realistic communication method. Explain why that combination can help people act on the issue. An impressive slogan or a large audience alone does not establish that a strategy fits the problem. Viability concerns whether the chosen approach is workable under the circumstances.

CS 3 • Check relevant expertise

Examine the writer’s or organization’s qualifications, experience and reputation in the subject being discussed. Expertise must relate to the claim. A skilled athlete is not automatically an expert on bridge engineering. Relevant expertise supports credibility, but the source’s evidence and reasoning must also be checked.

Back to guide contents ↑

CS 3 • Trace evidence to the conclusion

Ask what supports a claim and whether the reasoning goes beyond that support. Numerical claims should be traceable to appropriate data and methods. A limited observation may justify further investigation without proving a complete explanation or guaranteed result. A persuasive claim needs an evidence-based connection between its premises and conclusion.

CS 3 • Compare credible sources

Check a source against other credible information. When figures disagree, examine original records, dates, definitions, methods and populations before deciding whether there is a real conflict. Several websites copying the same unsupported claim do not provide independent confirmation.

Back to guide contents ↑

CS 3 • Examine date, purpose and circumstances

Ask when the source was created, why it was produced and who was expected to read it. Changing conditions can make an older source less useful for a current question without making it false when written. Campaign material and an audit have different purposes, but neither format alone settles whether a particular claim is accurate.

Back to guide contents ↑

CS 3 • Investigate interests and assumptions

Look for incentives, selective presentation and conclusions taken for granted. Financial or political interests may influence a message, but they do not automatically make every claim false. Check evidence and corroboration. An assertion that an entire population agrees needs support about that population rather than an assumption based on a few voices.

CS 3 • Match the source to the question

A credible source can still be irrelevant to a particular task. Match the topic, place, time period and kind of information needed. An adopted budget answers a question about authorized spending; a campaign proposal describes what a candidate would like to change. Distinguish current records from predictions or background information.

CS 3 • Test internal consistency

Compare statements within the same source. Incompatible claims about the same subject, measure and period need clarification. This is internal consistency. Agreement with other credible sources is a separate check. An inconsistency does not tell you by itself which of the conflicting statements is correct.

CS 4 • Persuasion

Persuasion tries to influence another person’s judgment through reasons, evidence or an appeal. A representative may explain why a proposal would address a concern. Persuasion can occur during negotiation or debate; it is not the same as exchanging concessions or reaching a group decision.

Back to guide contents ↑

CS 4 • Compromise

Compromise involves accepting concessions to reach an agreement. Participants accept less than all of their initial demands to secure a workable result. It can bridge different priorities, but it does not mean everyone gets everything or abandons all beliefs. A compromise still needs any required formal governmental approval.

Back to guide contents ↑

CS 4 • Consensus building

Consensus building works toward a decision participants can broadly accept by discussing and addressing concerns. It differs from ending disagreement through a narrow majority vote. Participants need not have identical preferences; they seek an outcome they can support or accept. Simply announcing a result without discussion does not build consensus.

Back to guide contents ↑

CS 4 • Negotiation

Negotiation is an exchange aimed at settling terms or resolving differences. Participants identify priorities, make offers, consider counteroffers and explore changes they could accept. Negotiation may use persuasion and may lead to compromise or consensus. The case’s specific actions help identify which skill is most directly illustrated.

Back to guide contents ↑

CS 4 • Skills working together

Governing often requires understanding different priorities and building support. A discussion can include persuasive arguments, negotiations over terms, compromises and attempts at consensus. Listening and revising can improve a proposal. These skills help resolve differences; they do not replace the formal procedures required to adopt or enforce public decisions.

CS 1 / CS 3 • Compare like measures

Define the measure before comparing figures. Total spending, cost per service and percentage change answer different questions. Cost per service equals spending divided by services delivered. A program can spend more in total while spending less per service if volume increases sufficiently. For risk measures, use a comparable denominator such as incidents per 1,000 users. Check time periods, units and conditions before declaring either success or a contradiction.

CS 2 • Evaluate outreach without overclaiming

Evaluate a communication strategy against its stated goal: reach, attendance, informed participation or support. These are different outcomes. Attendees who identify a message as their source provide evidence that it reached them. A before-and-after attendance increase may have several causes, so it does not prove that one message caused the entire change. Participation does not necessarily mean agreement with the organizer’s position.

Back to guide contents ↑

CS 3 • Read graph values and scales

Read the values, units, axes and time period before judging a graph’s visual impression. A percentage increase equals the change divided by the starting value, multiplied by 100. A narrow vertical range can make a small numerical change appear large. That emphasis deserves evaluation, but a nonzero axis does not automatically establish false data or improper presentation. A graph showing a change alone also does not establish what caused it.
